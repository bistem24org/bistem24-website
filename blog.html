More tips for STEM in the classroom

27/12/2023

0 Comments

 
#ScienceTeachers and #CuriousMinds!






​

​Ever wonder how to spark that scientific curiosity in students? The answer is simple: Start with an observation! 🌿🔬

Sharing some tips penned by my stem colleague Zachariah Mbasu (Lead PhET ambassador in Africa)
👇
The journey of scientific discovery begins with a keen eye and an inquisitive mind. And the best part? 

👉 Step 1: Encourage Observation Encourage your students to actively observe the world around them. Whether it's the way a plant grows or the mechanics of a swinging pendulum, every observation is a starting point for exploration. 👀🌳

👉 Step 2: Formulate Questions A good observation often leads to a great question. Ask students what they're curious about, and guide them in phrasing their queries effectively. ❓

👉 Step 3: Hypothesize Encourage them to come up with a testable hypothesis. This is a potential answer to the question that can be verified through experimentation. 🤔

👉 Step 4: Experiment! This is where the fun really begins! Students can engage in hands-on experiments to test their hypothesis. Make sure they take notes and gather data. 🧪📊

👉 Step 5: Analyze and Conclude After the experiment, help students analyze their data. What did they learn? Was their hypothesis correct? 📈🔍

Teaching science isn't just about memorizing facts; it's about instilling a mindset of curiosity and methodical inquiry. And the best time to start is NOW! ⏰

So go ahead, encourage your students to #ObserveAskExperiment and let the scientific journey begin! 🌟

Pedagogy Tips for using EdTech in the classroom.

27/12/2023

0 Comments

 
Starting out.... Pedagogy tips for technology use in the classroom. 

Today, there is much talk about Technology integration with education and learning. Particularly adapting and incorporating into classroom processes and procedures to create better outcomes– which could be to support teaching techniques, aid students’ retention, enhance engagement and drive higher attainments or generally enhance overall learning experiences. 

You would agree with me that the world where ‘9-5’ was the classical tradition for work, no longer exists! The face of work has changed and with it, opportunities and the evolution of aspects of human preferences. 

Marc Prensky, an acclaimed educational theorist, developed a term called ‘Digital natives’, he describes it as…”individuals who have grown up in a world surrounded by digital technology. They are accustomed to constant connectivity, multitasking, and interactive experiences. For these learners, traditional teaching methods that rely heavily on lectures and passive absorption of information may not effectively capture their attention or fully exploit their potential."- Paul Cook (MA PGCE BA Hons- Teacher Trainer, 2022). This mythical, but apt description, brings to mind one of Chinua Achebe’s (1930 - 2013) quotes that says…’hunters have Iearned to shoot without missing, so birds will learn to fly without perching…’ (my paraphrase). In summary, the traditional classroom needs a flip to accommodate and allow creative expressions, to enhance learning experiences, support students' progression, and thus improve the quality of teaching and learning. 

I’ll share a few pedagogic tips here to flip your teaching to allow more student involvement and make your classroom more active, fun and productive. 

1. Use visuals in your lessons and activity design!
If you can, employ more interactive visuals - the PhET Interactive Simulations, University of Colorado Boulder are great with offline access. 
Using visuals aids your students’ ability to think, reflect and create mentally. Interactive visuals allows manipulations and transformations that enables students uncover deeper layers of understanding and meaning from their own explorations. They can self explore a subject, structure or phenomenon, beyond natural restrictions. 
This kind of learning produces a deeper level of understanding and is more impactful than being told what to do or to merely ‘define’ a structure or term. 

2. Combine visuals with guide prompts. 
Asking an elementary student to define the word 'numerator' in maths, is a classic traditional teaching method which limits cognition, and the learning process to mere ‘head knowledge' and reception by rote.
Rather, use word prompts that promote engagement of psychomotor skills and kinesthetic movements, to promote critical thinking. E. g, foster more psychomotor engagement by encouraging variable manipulations in the fraction, and letting them observe, point to, describe what they see, and explain causes and effects.

Developing Spatial Skills: how the curriculum helps

8/9/2022

0 Comments

 
The ability to see and think with structures, is called ‘spatial visualization’. Spatial visualisation is useful in helping students see and analyse structure in phenomena and processes that are invisible traditionally, it also develops metacognitive processes that initiate scientific inquiry (Uttal & Ramay, 2017). 
Being able to draw conceptual model or path of a process helps students to unravel scientific problems. Conceptual modeling trains the mind to unravel structures within a system, this is why many educators use concept maps to encourage students to constructively breakdown learning contents into small parts that they can uniquely study to get the whole picture.
Spatial visualization is much the same way, and it can be learned. The 'Spatial Simulation curriculum' was developed to help Physics students understand the concept of forces, by applying Newtonian Principles through a constructive process supported with technology integration.







​

​Fig. 1- A model of the Spatial curriculum instructional cycle.
The curriculum employs a constructivist approach to metacognition and scientific inquiry so that students can own their learning, with the use of experimental models and insights from readings, and from interactions with other students 


Learning with computer simulations have become popular, with the advancement of educational technology, thus there are quite a number of different types of simulations, for various learning uses; however simulations should be carefully selected for particular task and learning processes. For example, in the learning of physics concepts with multi dimensional play of forces like we have in mechanics, simulations with embedded spatial properties have visual affordances and graphics that enhance spatial thinking and manipulations necessary for analyzing such systems. The HTML PhET Simulations is a notable example of a tested pedagogically grounded interactive simulation app, It uses vivid images of familiar situations that create unforgettable memory experiences for students, and has embedded spatial information.
Some people believe that using vivid images and sometimes games, oversimplifies or distracts from the essential objective of education, which is learning. However, studies on memory effect of images and multimodal media, show that people’s memory for images they interact with lasts longer- the load on the working memory is reduced,  this aids mental conceptual processes which develops understanding (Sweller, 2005; Bransford et al, 2000; Lindgren & Schwartz, 2009).
fig 2- showing images of some PhET HTML sims with graphics and spatial characteristics. 
using simulations and other multi modal interactive representations, aids students' metacognition, i.e, their ability to reflect and mentally visualize a phenomenon beyond what is taught. Simulations aid spatial visualization.
​
​Traditional physics curriculum emphasizes the mathematics of physics problem solving, where physical laws are represented by mathematical models – e.g, Newton’s second law of motion is represented as F= ma. However, the focus in the design of a spatial simulation curriculum, is not too much on the formulaic and mathematical aspects of Physics problem solving, but first on developing metacognitive and spatial skills that help students' mental conceptual processes- for example, a structural engineering student  with these skills mastery can visualize, manipulate and produce structures mentally.
 Following a curriculum designed to aid mental and spatial development ensures that students can  ‘learn how to learn’ abstract concepts.


References
